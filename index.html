Three problems, one symptom

Damp is a diagnosis, not a product.

A tide mark on a Glasgow tenement wall can be four different things. Injecting a chemical damp-proof course fixes exactly one of them. This is what we're separating when we survey.

Groundwater rising by capillary action

Rising damp

Genuinely uncommon, and over-diagnosed for fifty years. It stops at a horizontal tide line, usually under a metre, and carries ground salts that bloom through the plaster. On solid stone walls the cause is often a raised external ground level, a bridged DPC, or a blocked sub-floor vent — not a failed course.

Water crossing the wall from outside

Penetrating damp

The most common cause of a damp Scottish wall. Cracked harling, cement render trapping moisture in a lime-built wall, failed pointing, a leaking downpipe, a slipped slate, a defective chimney flashing. It appears at the point of failure and worsens after rain rather than following the seasons.

Moisture produced inside the building

Condensation & mould

Black spot mould in corners, behind furniture, on cold reveals and single-glazed margins. It is a heating, ventilation and cold-bridging problem, and no chemical treatment will touch it. This is the category most often reported by tenants, and the one Awaab's Law is written around.

How we tell them apart

Evidence, not opinion

A number on a meter is not a diagnosis.

That reading tells you the wall is wet. It does not tell you why, and a conductivity meter will read salt, foil-backed board and metal lath just as enthusiastically as it reads water.

What turns a reading into a diagnosis is the pattern across the elevation, the height it stops at, whether it worsens after rain or in winter, the salts in the plaster, and what the outside of that wall is doing. That is the work. The meter is just where it starts.

Surveys

What a proper survey
actually involves.

Ninety minutes, a moisture meter, a hygrometer, a borescope, a ladder, and someone who has cut open enough Scottish walls to know what they're looking at.

On site

Internal moisture profile

Readings at skirting level, 300 mm and 1 m on every affected elevation, plotted so the pattern is visible rather than asserted. Surface readings are cross-checked with deep-wall probes, because a conductivity meter reads salt and foil as enthusiastically as it reads water.

On site

Air and surface conditions

Relative humidity, air temperature and surface temperature at the cold spots. This is what distinguishes condensation from moisture arriving through the fabric, and it is the measurement most "free surveys" skip entirely.

On site

External defect inspection

Ground levels against the wall, air brick and sub-floor ventilation, pointing and harling condition, rainwater goods, chimney heads and flashings, and any cement render sitting on a wall that was built to breathe.

On site

Timber and sub-floor

Joist ends where they bear into damp masonry, wall plates, lintels and bearings, plus a lifted board or borescope inspection of the solum where access allows. Rot is found under floors far more often than on them.

Where needed

Salt analysis

Nitrate and chloride testing on drilled samples. Ground salts confirm rising damp; their absence is often the single fact that saves a client several thousand pounds.

Afterwards

The report

Photographic, room by room, with the diagnosis and the evidence for it, then a schedule of works split into urgent, remedial and general maintenance — costed, so you can price it against anyone else's quote.

Damp

Telling them apart.

The pattern, the height, the timing and the salts. Four pieces of evidence that between them account for nearly every damp wall in Scotland.

Diagnosis

Rising damp

  • Defined horizontal tide line, typically 0.5–1.2 m
  • Hygroscopic salts present in the plaster
  • Constant through the year rather than after rain
  • Often traced to raised external ground, a bridged or rendered-over DPC, or a blocked sub-floor vent

Where a chemical DPC is genuinely warranted we install one and re-plaster to a salt-retardant specification. Where it isn't, we say so.

Diagnosis

Penetrating damp

  • Localised patch, any height, worst after driving rain
  • Traceable to a defect: cracked harling, open joints, failed pointing, blocked or split downpipe, slipped slate, chimney flashing
  • Cement render or plastic paint on a lime-built wall, holding water in
  • Exposed west-coast elevations taking the brunt of it

The fix is nearly always building repair rather than chemical treatment — which is why our report tells you what to instruct, not what to buy from us.

Diagnosis

Condensation & mould

  • Black spot growth in corners, on cold reveals, behind furniture and inside wardrobes on external walls
  • Worst in winter, worst in bedrooms and bathrooms
  • Driven by cold surfaces, moisture production and too little ventilation
  • Frequently made worse by sealing up an old building

We measure surface temperatures and humidity, identify the cold bridges, and specify ventilation and heating improvements. No chemical treatment resolves condensation.

Diagnosis

Plumbing & defect leaks

  • Sharp, contained, often below a bathroom or kitchen
  • Flat roofs, box gutters, valley gutters and shared close roofs
  • Ingress from the flat above in tenement property
  • Frequently mislabelled as rising damp because it presents low down

Identifying the true source matters commercially as well as technically: it determines whether the cost sits with you, a neighbour, a factor or an insurer.

Timber decay

Rot doesn't stop
where you can see it.

Dry rot is the one Scottish tenements are notorious for, and the one that costs the most when it is found late. Wet rot is more common and far more manageable. Woodworm is usually historic and usually over-treated.

Serpula lacrymans

Dry rot

Rust-red spore dust, cuboidal cracking across the grain, grey-white mycelium and strands that can travel through masonry and behind plaster to reach dry timber several rooms away. It needs a persistent water source — find and stop that first, or the treatment is decoration.

We establish the full extent by opening up, cut back to sound timber with an agreed margin, sterilise the masonry where justified, and specify the building repair that removes the moisture.

Coniophora and others

Wet rot

Darkened, soft, stringy timber confined to the wet area. Joist ends built into a damp gable, window cills, sub-floor plates, the bottom of a door frame. Contained, predictable, and resolved by stopping the water and replacing or resin-repairing the affected timber.

Anobium punctatum

Woodworm

Common furniture beetle in damp sub-floor softwood. The question is never "are there holes" — it is whether the infestation is active, which we assess by frass, bore hole condition and timber moisture content. Historic infestation in dry timber needs no chemical treatment at all.

Scottish building stock is regional. So is the way it fails.

Glasgow & the west

Blonde and red sandstone tenements

Rubble-cored walls with an ashlar face, shared closes and back courts. Failed common downpipes, defective bay window roofs, and cement pointing trapping water in walls built for lime. Dry rot in bay corners and close ceilings is the recurring find.

Edinburgh & the Lothians

Ashlar, basements and drying greens

Sunken basement and lower-ground flats with high external ground levels and poor solum ventilation. Chimney head and skew failures on tall stacks. Retentions on Home Reports are common in the older tenement stock.

Aberdeen & the north east

Granite

Dense, unforgiving walls where the moisture sits at the joints. Pointing failure, harling breakdown on rear elevations and driven rain on exposed coastal frontages. Condensation is severe where solid granite meets modern airtightness.

Highlands, Argyll & the islands

Harled solid walls, high exposure

Rainfall and wind-driven saturation dominate. Harling cracks, breather membranes and vent paths matter more than any chemical treatment. We plan island and west coast work in batches — ask about scheduling.

Dundee, Fife & Perthshire

Mixed stock

Sandstone terraces alongside interwar and post-war cavity housing, where the failure is usually a bridged cavity, debris on wall ties, or an added conservatory raising ground levels above the DPC.

Borders & Dumfries

Rubble walls and red sandstone

Thick, wide-jointed rubble walls that hold water for weeks after a wet spell, and inappropriate modern renders that have sealed them. Sub-floor rot in older cottages with earth or slab-covered solums.

Landlords, agents, factors & housing associations

Ten working days, starting 6 October 2026.

Scotland's version of Awaab's Law is made by the Investigation and Commencement of Repair (Scotland) Regulations 2026, approved by the Scottish Parliament in March 2026 and in force from 6 October 2026. Unlike the first phase in England, it applies to the private rented sector as well as social housing.

Where the damp or mould is not the result of a building defect, landlords are expected to work with tenants and signpost appropriate advice and guidance.

The wider legal picture

What already applied
before October.

The new timescales sit on top of duties that have been in force for years. Damp and mould were never optional.

Under the Housing (Scotland) Act 2006, a private rented property must be wind and watertight and in all other respects reasonably fit for human habitation, at the start of the tenancy and throughout it. The 2026 regulations amend the standard so that a property must be substantially free from damp and mould, and add the investigation and commencement timescales above.

Enforcement is through the First-tier Tribunal for Scotland (Housing and Property Chamber). The Tribunal can make a Repairing Standard Enforcement Order, and can make a rent relief order reducing the rent payable. Failure to comply with an enforcement order is a serious matter for a registered landlord.

Housing (Scotland) Act 2006 · Investigation and Commencement of Repair (Scotland) Regulations 2026

The Tolerable Standard under the Housing (Scotland) Act 1987 is the minimum condition below which a house is not fit to live in. It includes being substantially free from rising damp and penetrating damp. It applies across tenures and is the standard local authorities use when taking action on individual houses.

Housing (Scotland) Act 1987, s.86

Scottish secure tenants have a statutory right to have certain small urgent repairs, costing under £350, carried out within set timescales — commonly 1, 3 or 7 working days depending on the repair — with compensation payable where the landlord misses the deadline and the tenant can instruct a second contractor from the landlord's list.

The scheme was not written around damp and mould specifically, and most damp remediation falls outside it on cost. It matters here mainly because the associated defects — a blocked drain, a leaking pipe, an unsafe or defective vent — often do qualify.

Scottish Secure Tenants (Right to Repair) Regulations 2002

In tenement property the defect causing damp is frequently in shared fabric: the roof, a gable, the close, a common downpipe. Who decides, and who pays, is governed first by the title deeds and, where the deeds are silent or make no provision, by the Tenement Management Scheme in the Tenements (Scotland) Act 2004 — which allows a majority of owners to make a scheme decision on necessary maintenance to scheme property.

A landlord's repairing duty does not evaporate because the defect is common property. Our reports locate the defect precisely, which is what factors and other owners need before agreeing to fund a repair.

Tenements (Scotland) Act 2004

Social landlords are additionally answerable to the Scottish Housing Regulator and report against the Scottish Social Housing Charter and the Scottish Housing Quality Standard. The 2026 duties add fixed, auditable timescales to what was previously governed by internal repairs policies, so investigation records, dated written summaries and evidence of work commencing become the compliance trail.

Where a Single Survey records dampness, a lender may hold a retention until a specialist report is produced. This affects landlords buying and selling stock as much as it affects homeowners. A dated specialist report with a costed schedule of works is normally what releases the funds.

Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 our services must be carried out with reasonable care and skill, within a reasonable time, for a reasonable price where none has been agreed. Where a contract is agreed at your home rather than at our premises, the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 give you a 14-day right to cancel, and we will give you that notice in writing before you sign anything.

Note also that Scotland's prescriptive period differs from England's: under the Prescription and Limitation (Scotland) Act 1973 most contractual obligations prescribe after five years, not six. It is worth knowing where guarantees and defect claims are concerned.
